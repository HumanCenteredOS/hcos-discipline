## Slide 1 — The Future of AI May Feel Like One Relationship

Welcome. In this activity, we will use a Human-Centered Operating Systems, or HCOS™, lens to explore a practical question that is becoming increasingly important for healthcare professionals.

HCOS is a way of examining technology, workflows, policies, and organizational decisions as parts of a larger human system. It begins with a simple idea: technology should support people, protect human dignity, reduce unnecessary burden, and help professionals make sound decisions. Rather than looking only at whether a tool works technically, HCOS also asks whether the surrounding system is understandable, trustworthy, appropriately governed, and safe for the people who depend on it.

The specific question we will consider is this: if artificial intelligence begins to feel like one continuous assistant—following a person across devices, tasks, and settings—what must be true underneath that experience for it to remain safe, trustworthy, and clinically appropriate?

This is not a technical build session, and it is not a prediction that one AI system will control every aspect of care. Instead, it is an architectural forecast and educational insight. We will examine the possibility that a unified AI experience may actually depend on many specialized systems working together behind the scenes.

For healthcare, that raises important questions. How does the system know who the person is? Which role or clinical context is active? What information may be remembered? What actions may the AI take? Who remains accountable when multiple systems participate in a decision or workflow?

Throughout the activity, we will consider identity, context, memory, authority, delegation, and responsibility as human-centered design questions. The goal is not to provide all of the technical answers. The goal is to help healthcare professionals recognize the governance questions that must be addressed before increasingly persistent and coordinated AI systems can be used responsibly.

## Slide 4 — Learning Objectives

By the end of this activity, you should be able to distinguish a smooth or continuous AI experience from the many systems that may be operating underneath it.

You should also be able to explain why identity, context, delegated authority, and governed memory matter when AI participates in healthcare work.

We will look at what can happen when information or authority moves across patient, clinician, caregiver, payer, pharmacy, and organizational settings without preserving the original context.

Most importantly, you should leave with a practical way to review an AI-supported workflow before it moves from providing information to performing an action.

## Slide 5 — Educational Scope and Disclaimer

This activity is intended to support professional education and critical thinking. It does not provide legal, regulatory, privacy, cybersecurity, billing, compliance, or patient-specific clinical advice.

The examples are intentionally simplified so that we can see the architectural questions more clearly. In practice, every organization will need to adapt these ideas to its own policies, workflows, patient populations, professional scopes of practice, security controls, and applicable laws.

AI should not be permitted to make consequential patient-specific decisions or perform healthcare actions simply because the technology is capable of doing so. Appropriate validation, authorization, human review, documentation, and accountability remain necessary.

Throughout the activity, the goal is not to expand AI authority. The goal is to make the boundaries around that authority understandable and intentional.

## Slide 6 — Why This Matters in Healthcare

Healthcare already depends on many identities, roles, systems, and permissions.

A patient may interact with clinicians, pharmacists, caregivers, health plans, pharmacies, laboratories, scheduling systems, portals, and hospitals. The same information may move through several of those settings, but it does not always have the same meaning or the same permitted use in each one.

AI may eventually help coordinate across these spaces. That could reduce repeated work and make care easier to navigate. But continuity is not the same as permission.

A system can feel convenient while still being unsafe if it confuses roles, applies information outside its original context, remembers something that is no longer true, or acts with more authority than the person intended to give it.

---

## Slide 7 — Introducing the HCOS™ Lens

HCOS stands for Human-Centered Operating Systems.

It is a way of looking at technology as one part of a larger human system. That larger system also includes the workflow, policies, staffing, resources, incentives, leadership decisions, communication pathways, and daily operating conditions around the technology.

The HCOS question is not only, “Does the AI work?” It also asks, “What happens to the people who must rely on it, supervise it, correct it, and remain accountable for what happens next?”

A human-centered system should support people, preserve dignity and agency, reduce unnecessary burden, and keep responsibility visible. This lens is especially important in healthcare because the impact of a technical decision may ultimately be experienced by a patient, family member, or healthcare professional.

---

## Slide 8 — From Apps to Goals

Today, most of us still interact with technology by choosing an application. We open email, calendar, maps, banking, the electronic health record, or a patient portal.

The forecast explored in this activity is that AI may gradually change the interface from choosing a tool to expressing a goal.

Instead of deciding which application should be opened first, a person may simply say, “Help me plan my daughter’s birthday.”

The visible interaction becomes simpler. The person expresses an intention, and the system coordinates the supporting work. But the simplicity of the request should not lead us to assume that the underlying architecture is equally simple.

---

## Slide 9 — A Simple Personal AI Scenario

Consider what may happen after that single request.

The system may check calendars, identify possible venues, draft invitations, coordinate with family members, update a budget, and schedule reminders.

From the person’s perspective, it may feel like one relationship with one assistant. Underneath, the request may involve several models, applications, databases, communication services, organizations, and permission boundaries.

This example is intentionally ordinary. It allows us to see the architectural pattern before we apply it to a healthcare workflow where the consequences may be much greater.

---

## Slide 10 — A Healthcare AI Request Is Rarely One Task

Now consider a healthcare request: “Help this patient start therapy safely and on time.”

That appears to be one goal, but it may involve clinical criteria, documentation, benefit coverage, prior authorization, laboratory monitoring, patient education, pharmacy coordination, scheduling, and follow-up.

Each part of the workflow may require different information, professional roles, organizational systems, and levels of authority.

This is where the healthcare question becomes more precise. What may the AI see? What may it remember? What may it summarize or recommend? What may it draft? What may it send, submit, modify, or initiate?

Those actions are not interchangeable. The level of governance should increase as the AI moves closer to consequential action.

---

## Slide 11 — Knowledge Check 1: Which Safeguard Matters Most?

Let’s pause for the first knowledge check.

A healthcare AI is coordinating follow-up across scheduling, pharmacy, and insurance systems. Which safeguard matters most?

Take a moment to consider the difference between making the workflow feel continuous and giving every participating system broad access.

The question is not asking which option would make the process fastest. It is asking which option best protects the patient while allowing the work to be coordinated.

---

## Slide 12 — Knowledge Check 1: Answer and Explanation

The best answer is C: limit each capability to the identity, context, and authority needed for its task.

A continuous experience should not mean unlimited access. A scheduling function may need different information than a clinical review function, and a drafting function may require less authority than a system that submits or changes something.

The practical safeguard is to use the minimum information and authority needed for the specific task. Retrieval, drafting, communication, submission, and decision-making should remain distinguishable.

Continuity should reduce unnecessary burden without collapsing the permission boundaries that protect privacy, safety, and accountability.

---

## Slide 13 — What Changes When AI Coordinates Work?

The next step is to recognize that AI actions exist at different levels of consequence.

An AI may inform, recommend, draft, communicate, initiate, or complete an action. Those verbs may sound similar in casual conversation, but they represent very different levels of authority.

For example, explaining prior-authorization requirements is not the same as submitting an authorization. Drafting a patient message is not the same as sending it. Identifying a possible medication issue is not the same as changing an order.

As AI moves from information toward action, the need for identity verification, permission, review, auditability, and accountability increases.

---

## Slide 14 — One Goal, Different Types of Authority

A single goal should not receive one broad permission.

Within the same specialty-medication workflow, an AI might retrieve and summarize clinical information, prepare documentation, draft a message, submit a transaction, or offer decision support.

Each task should be governed separately. A system may be permitted to identify missing information without being allowed to contact the patient. It may be permitted to draft documentation without being allowed to release it. It may support a clinical review without being permitted to make the professional decision.

The teaching point is that authority should be assigned by task. The organization should not treat the entire workflow as one undifferentiated block of permission.

---

## Slide 15 — The Five Questions Before AI Acts

This slide provides a practical review method.

Before AI-supported work moves from information to action, ask five questions.

First, who is acting? Is the AI representing the patient, clinician, pharmacy, payer, health system, or another organization?

Second, what is the purpose? What specific goal is the system pursuing in this moment?

Third, what information is actually needed? Is access limited to the minimum information required for this task?

Fourth, what may the AI do? May it retrieve, summarize, recommend, draft, send, submit, modify, or decide?

Fifth, what review is required? Who must confirm the action, and who remains accountable for what happens next?

These questions can be used by clinical teams, operational leaders, informaticists, compliance professionals, and implementation teams even when they are not designing the underlying technology themselves.

## Slide 16 — Knowledge Check 2: Drafting Is Not the Same as Sending

Here is the second knowledge check.

An AI drafts a patient message about missing laboratory work and then sends the message automatically. What is the most important governance concern?

Focus on what changed between preparing the text and releasing it to the patient.

The content may be accurate and the language may be appropriate, but the system has crossed from drafting into performing a communication on behalf of a healthcare professional or organization.

---

## Slide 17 — Knowledge Check 2: Answer and Explanation

The best answer is B: sending is a delegated action that requires defined authority and review.

A draft prepares communication. Sending performs communication.

Once the message is released, the patient may reasonably believe that it represents an authorized clinician, pharmacy, or healthcare organization. The system therefore needs a clearly defined identity, communication authority, review threshold, and escalation pathway.

The appropriate review may vary based on the content and consequence. A routine scheduling reminder is different from a message that interprets laboratory results or changes a treatment plan. The governance should match the possible impact on the patient.

---

## Slide 18 — When Context Collapses

Some AI failures may look like reasoning errors when the deeper problem is a boundary failure.

A caregiver’s preference may be mistaken for the patient’s preference. Information from an earlier encounter may be treated as current. A draft recommendation may be interpreted as an approved clinical decision. An AI acting for the organization may appear to be speaking for an individual clinician.

The same fact can have a different meaning in a different context. A statement may have been true during one hospitalization, for one purpose, or under one temporary permission without being appropriate for every future encounter.

Context collapse occurs when those distinctions disappear. Preventing it requires systems that preserve the source, role, time, purpose, and authority connected to the information or action.

---

## Slide 19 — Governed Memory in Practice

Memory can make an AI relationship more useful. It can reduce repeated explanations, preserve preferences, and support continuity across time.

But healthcare professionals also understand that memory can become a source of harm when information is incorrect, outdated, incomplete, or used outside its original purpose.

For every remembered item, ask: Where did it come from? In what context was it true? Was persistence or reuse appropriate? Can the patient or professional correct it? When should it expire or be reconfirmed? Can we see where it influenced a later decision or action?

Governed memory is not simply a larger database. It is memory that carries its meaning, limits, history, and correction pathway with it.

---

## Slide 20 — Human Review Is Not One Thing

The phrase “human in the loop” can sound reassuring, but it does not tell us what the human is actually expected to do.

Not every AI action requires the same checkpoint. Low-consequence support, such as formatting or general education, may require lighter review. Drafting patient communication or routing clinical information may require a more deliberate checkpoint. Submitting an authorization, modifying an order, or communicating a treatment recommendation may require direct professional confirmation.

Human review should be matched to consequence, uncertainty, reversibility, and professional responsibility.

The goal is not to require a person to reperform every automated task. That would simply transfer the burden back to the workforce. The goal is to place meaningful human judgment where it can still prevent or correct harm.

---

## Slide 21 — Knowledge Check 3: Where Is the Boundary Failure?

For the third knowledge check, consider this scenario.

An AI reviews the record, drafts an authorization, messages the prescriber, and submits the request before the pharmacist completes the clinical review.

What is the primary governance problem?

Notice that the issue is not necessarily whether the AI used several systems or whether the draft itself was accurate. The question is whether the system crossed the defined action boundary before the required review occurred.

---

## Slide 22 — Knowledge Check 3: Answer and Explanation

The best answer is B: the AI exceeded delegated authority before the review threshold was met.

Capability is not permission.

The system may be capable of retrieving information and preparing a draft, but submission is a delegated transaction. It represents an action taken within a healthcare, payer, or pharmacy process and may affect the patient’s access to care.

A responsible human or an approved governance pathway must define when that action can occur, what evidence is required, how exceptions are handled, and who remains accountable.

---

## Slide 23 — What Healthcare Organizations Can Do Now

Healthcare organizations do not need to predict the entire future before beginning this work.

Start with one real AI-enabled workflow. Map every person, system, and organization involved. Separate retrieval, drafting, communication, decision, and action rather than treating them as one automated process.

Define the information each task actually requires. Establish authority and review thresholds before deployment. Name the accountable human or organizational owner. Create correction, escalation, and audit pathways.

Finally, evaluate whether the AI truly reduces human burden or simply moves invisible checking, cleanup, and accountability onto someone else.

The purpose of the map is not to slow innovation. It is to make the hidden architecture visible enough to govern responsibly.

## Slide 24 — Practical Takeaway and Credit Claiming

As you leave this activity, take six questions back to your own setting.

Who is the AI representing? What context is active? What information is necessary? What authority has been delegated? What review is required? Who remains responsible?

Those questions can be applied to a patient-facing assistant, documentation tool, clinical decision-support system, access workflow, prior-authorization process, scheduling function, or operational agent.

The future of AI may feel like one continuous relationship. Healthy healthcare systems will make sure that the continuity supports the person without erasing the boundaries that protect identity, privacy, professional judgment, and accountability.
