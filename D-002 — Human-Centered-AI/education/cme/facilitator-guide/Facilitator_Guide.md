# Facilitator Guide

## The Future of AI May Feel Like One Relationship

**Activity type:** HCOS™ Insight and Architectural Forecast adapted for CME / CE  
**Audience:** Healthcare professionals  
**Version:** 1.1

## Purpose of This Guide

This guide supports faculty members who are presenting the activity live, recording an enduring activity, or adapting the material for facilitated discussion.

The activity is designed to help healthcare professionals recognize that a simple or continuous AI experience may depend on many specialized systems operating across different identities, contexts, memories, permissions, and levels of authority.

The faculty member does not need to be a software architect. The primary role of the facilitator is to help learners connect the architectural concepts to healthcare workflow, professional responsibility, and patient impact.

## Central Teaching Message

> **The future of AI may feel like one continuous relationship, but healthy systems must preserve boundaries among identity, context, memory, authority, capability, and responsibility.**

## Audience Framing

At the beginning, acknowledge that many learners may not be familiar with HCOS™.

Describe HCOS as a human-centered systems lens that examines technology together with the surrounding workflow, policy, staffing, resources, incentives, leadership decisions, and operating conditions.

Avoid presenting HCOS™ terminology as established scientific consensus. Identify it as original conceptual work used to organize human-centered questions about system design and stewardship.

## Faculty Tone

Use a calm, conversational, and exploratory tone.

Recommended framing:

- “Here is a question healthcare teams may need to ask.”
- “This example helps us see where the boundary changes.”
- “The point is not to assume that AI should perform the action.”
- “The goal is to make the authority and accountability visible.”
- “Different organizations may make different decisions based on risk and context.”

Avoid:

- implying that the forecast is certain;
- suggesting that one architecture is appropriate for every organization;
- presenting HCOS™ as a regulatory requirement;
- implying that human review automatically makes an AI workflow safe;
- or treating all AI-supported actions as equally consequential.

## Suggested Opening Question

Ask learners:

> Think about one AI-enabled workflow in your organization. Does the AI only provide information, or can it communicate, submit, modify, or initiate something?

This question prepares learners to notice the movement from information toward action.

## Knowledge-Check Facilitation

### Knowledge Check 1

Invite learners to choose an answer before revealing the explanation.

Ask:

> What could go wrong if every system received the complete patient record simply because the overall experience was intended to feel continuous?

Reinforce that continuity should not eliminate minimum-necessary access, contextual separation, or task-specific authority.

### Knowledge Check 2

Ask:

> At what exact moment did the AI move from assisting with communication to performing communication?

Reinforce that drafting and sending are separate actions. Sending represents an authorized person or organization and may create clinical, legal, operational, or relational consequences.

### Knowledge Check 3

Ask:

> Which part of this workflow could reasonably occur before pharmacist review, and which part should wait?

Reinforce that retrieval and drafting may be appropriate within one authority boundary, while submission or other consequential transactions may require a higher review threshold.

## Optional Discussion Prompts

Use one or two prompts depending on time:

1. Where could contextual identity become confused in your organization?
2. What information does an AI-supported workflow truly need, and what information is merely available?
3. Which AI actions in your environment are reversible, and which create consequences that are difficult to undo?
4. Who corrects the system when remembered information is wrong?
5. Does the current workflow reduce human burden, or does it create invisible review and cleanup work?
6. Who is accountable when several systems and organizations participate in one patient-facing outcome?

## Facilitating Sensitive Discussion

Learners may raise concerns about job displacement, surveillance, privacy, liability, bias, or institutional pressure to adopt AI.

Acknowledge the concern without dismissing it or making promises that the activity cannot support.

A useful response is:

> That concern is part of the system being examined. HCOS asks not only whether the technology can perform the task, but also how the surrounding system affects patients, professionals, workload, dignity, oversight, and responsibility.

## Practical Application Exercise

Invite learners to select one workflow and complete the following map:

| Review question | Workflow response |
|---|---|
| Who is the AI representing? | |
| What context is active? | |
| What is the specific purpose? | |
| What information is required? | |
| What may the AI retrieve or summarize? | |
| What may the AI draft? | |
| What may the AI send, submit, modify, or initiate? | |
| What review is required at each step? | |
| Who remains accountable? | |
| How can the patient or professional correct an error? | |
| How is the action audited? | |
| Does the workflow reduce or transfer human burden? | |

## Closing the Activity

End by returning to the six practical questions:

1. Who is the AI representing?
2. What context is active?
3. What information is necessary?
4. What authority has been delegated?
5. What review is required?
6. Who remains responsible?

Remind learners to complete the required post-test, evaluation, and credit-claiming process.
